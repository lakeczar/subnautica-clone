## Question

How should an opening event explain arrival and strand the player, and how should research settlements, scanned entries, personal journals and environmental evidence reveal a coherent mystery during the agreed campaign? Decide the central player question and revelation structure before commissioning prose. A lore worker may own a shared chronology and facts, while locations, blueprints and gameplay consequences must be coordinated with world/progression owners.

## Comments

The user selected: "The player wakes in a damaged pod with missing memories." This fixes the opening premise, not its explanation, the cause of memory loss, the player's identity or the mystery's ending. Those remain open. Scan entries, personal journals and environmental evidence should gradually reveal what happened.

The owner answered the atmosphere questionnaire: **"Wonder first, with tense depths and comforting refuges."** This fixes the emotional direction for creature design, lighting, sound and threat escalation. The art study depicts a shallow refuge region; deeper threat designs and survival pressure are still undecided. Preserve curiosity as the leading motivation, with safety in the pod/inhabited vessels and readable tension deeper down.

## Interview round

Following selection of the richer cel art direction, the integrated questionnaire asks three independent story decisions:

1. Central mystery: a living ocean memory network (recommended), failing ancient ocean machinery, or a fractured human expedition. These are proposed premises, not canon. The recommendation connects unusual biology and resources to the damaged-pod/missing-memory opening.
2. Player's forgotten role: a costly rescue decision (recommended), responsibility for causing the disaster, or arrival after the disaster. Do not assign an identity, culpability or ending until the owner answers.
3. Discovery guidance: leads to biomes/landmarks (recommended), explicit next-location objectives, or open investigation with few markers and more deduction. This will shape evidence placement and how discoveries guide expedition preparation.

Recommendations are not selections. Exact lore, chronology, characters and revelation sequence follow the owner's answers. The selected art style changes presentation, not the unresolved story premise.

## Answer

The owner delegates the central truth and protagonist's past to the narrative team rather than selecting one of the suggested premises:

- Mystery: "Surprise me, and try to let the Lore be ambiguous I try and figure out to s big reveal".
- Forgotten role: "Surprise me and make it some what shocking".
- Guidance: "Regional leads: clues suggest a biome or landmark; I find the site and connect the evidence."

This resolves the creative brief: author a coherent hidden chronology and a personally consequential, surprising reveal; let the player interpret ambiguous early evidence, then deliver a supported payoff. It does **not** select the previously recommended living network or rescue-decision premise. The narrative team has authority to choose the truth without asking the owner to spoil it for themselves.

The [story design contract](../../../docs/STORY_DESIGN.md) records the spoiler-free treatment, fair-clue requirements and regional guidance behavior. The [writer appendix — major story spoilers](../../../docs/spoilers/NARRATIVE_CANON.md) holds the actual canon, chronology, four character perspectives and fifteen linked clues. Keep the reveal out of chat summaries, PR descriptions and the main build input shown to the owner; the build input can explicitly reference the spoiler appendix for implementation agents. The public repository is not a private store: spoiler labels help the owner choose what to read.

The decision is resolved, while authoring and checking the canon against the world remain planning work. Exact regional names, route distances, vehicle requirements and resource costs remain the geography/fleet decision's responsibility. A later build must validate whether players understand the clues and find the reveal satisfying; this interview is not that evidence.
